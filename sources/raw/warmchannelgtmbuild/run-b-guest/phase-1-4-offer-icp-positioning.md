The guest side. A named executive at a Canadian bank, credit union or insurer, who pays nothing and is not buying anything.

## The vocabulary rule, and it governs this whole folder

`GTM-BRIEF.md` Section 5 sets it and it is absolute. **Client-facing copy never reaches a guest-facing surface.** A guest who reads the Run A language learns they were inventory, and the guest list is the only asset this business has.

The trust-scarcity argument is the sharpest case. Run A says that trust in digital channels is declining and in-person contact benefits. Said to a guest, that argument tells them their own professional presence is noise and their evening is a workaround for someone else's marketing problem.

In this folder, **analysis sections are internal** and use whatever words are accurate. **Sections marked guest-facing are copy** and are held to the guest vocabulary. The two are never merged into one document that leaves the building.

## Why Run B is not the second half of Run A

Run B is the supply side. Run A cannot be delivered without it, and guest-side inventory, not the method, is the asset that compounds. Being second in sequence does not make it second in importance.

The healthcare guest list is out of scope, per open decision 6, and the ground is consent rather than brand. Guests who consented to invitations from a healthcare convening business did not consent to invitations from a fintech vendor's dinner. **Guest-side inventory in this vertical starts at zero.**

## Phase 1. What the guest is actually offered

Six vectors again, redefined for a party that pays nothing. Guest Demand replaces client demand. Differentiation is against the other things competing for the same evening. Effort is ours. Acceptance Motion replaces sales motion. Time Cost replaces ticket size. Funnel Role is measured in inventory built, not revenue.

| # | Item | Guest Demand | Differentiation | Our Effort | Acceptance Motion | Their Time Cost | Funnel Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The question.** One live problem genuinely shared by everyone in the room | Highest in the set. It is the only reason a senior person gives up an evening | High. Most vendor rooms have a theme, not a question, and the difference is obvious within ten minutes | High. Requires real research into what is live for these institutions right now | Named in the first line of the invitation | None to evaluate | The entire acceptance decision |
| 2 | **The peers.** Who else is in the room, described by role and institution type | Very high, and it is what they ask about second | Medium. Everyone claims this. Few can show it before the date | High, and circular. The first acceptances are the hardest | Disclosed by role, never by name, before anyone has accepted | None | The compounding mechanism. Each acceptance makes the next easier |
| 3 | **The format.** Small, closed, no stage time, no pitching from the floor | High, and rising. Peer-only and pitch-free framing is now the category standard claim | Low as a claim, high as a practice. The claim is everywhere and the practice is not | Medium. It is mostly a set of refusals, enforced on the night | Stated plainly and kept | None | Trust, and it is what makes a second acceptance possible |
| 4 | **Known time cost.** One evening, a stated start and end, no follow-on obligation | Medium high, and underrated. Senior people decline on uncertainty as often as on content | High. Hosted-buyer operators do the opposite, binding delegates to meeting counts with penalties for leaving early | Low | Stated in the invitation | The evening | Removes the most common quiet reason for declining |
| 5 | **Discretion.** No attendee list circulated, no contact details handed to anyone, no follow-up sales sequence | High once they think about it, and most will not raise it | High. It is refused in the Run A scope, which means it can be promised here without strain | Low. It is a policy | Stated, and it is the most persuasive line available | None | The single largest determinant of whether they accept twice |
| 6 | **The summary.** A written account of what was discussed, if they want it | Medium | Low | Medium | Offered afterwards | None | Value delivered after the fact, which is the cheapest reason to accept again |

**What comes out of this.** Items 1 and 2 win the first acceptance. Items 3 and 5 win the second, and the second is worth more, because a guest who returns is inventory and a guest who attends once is a cost.

**Item 5 is the item to build the whole guest offer around, and it is free.** The Warm Channel already refuses attendee data handover in the Run A scope, for competitive reasons. That refusal, said to a guest, is the strongest promise available to a business with nothing else to offer them.

## Phase 2. The competitive set

The guest is not buying, so the competition is everything else competing for the same evening.

| Slot | What | Why they choose it over us | Why they might not |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A competing vendor's dinner | Known brand, better venue, a peer they already know is going | Expects to be pitched. Has been before |
| 2 | An industry association event | Institutional standing, no vendor attached, counts as professional development | Large, formal, the same faces, little said honestly |
| 3 | A peer-only network with no vendor present | The format they actually want. No agenda but their own | Hard to get into, and often does not exist in their specific function |
| 4 | Their own calendar, and their family | Costs nothing, and the default | Nothing. This wins most evenings and should be assumed to |
| 5 | A webinar that costs nothing to skip | Free, no travel, can be ignored | Worth exactly what it costs |

**Slot 4 is the real competitor and it is undefeated most of the time.** Every guest-facing decision should be read against going home instead, not against another dinner.

**The finding from the research pass, and its limit.** Vendor-event fatigue is documented in cybersecurity, where more than five thousand vendors compete and 68% of CISOs report that aggressive marketing makes genuine innovation hard to identify. **No equivalent evidence was found for Canadian banking, credit union or insurance executives.** That is an absence of data, not evidence of an absence of fatigue. Assume the fatigue is present until someone asks.

## Phase 3. Positioning, guest-facing

Structure borrowed from Run A. **Copy borrowed from nothing.**

### Statement 1. Against another vendor's dinner

**Not an evening with a sponsor. An evening with the eight people dealing with the same thing you are.**

There is a host, and the host is named in the invitation. There is no stage time, no deck, and no pitching from the floor. The question is set before anyone is invited, and it is set from what is actually live at institutions like yours rather than from what the host sells.

### Statement 2. Against an association event or a conference

**Not a room of two hundred. A table of twelve, on one question.**

Large rooms are for hearing what the industry is saying. This is for saying what you actually think, in front of people who will understand it and will not repeat it. Nobody is on a panel and nobody is presenting.

### Statement 3. Against staying home

**One evening, a known start and a known end, and nothing asked of you afterwards.**

No follow-up sequence. No contact details passed to anyone. No list of who was there circulated. If the evening is not worth your time you will know inside twenty minutes, and you will not hear from us about it again.

### What is never said to a guest

Composed attendance. Access. Inventory. Target account. In-ICP. Show rate. Attendance floor. Coverage. Anything about trust in digital channels declining, anything about the cost of reaching a buyer, and anything that describes the guest's presence as a thing the host bought.

## Phase 4. Guest ICP

The skill's two levels read here as position in the room rather than stage of a company.

| Attribute | Level 1, the accessible guest | Level 2, the anchor guest |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Director or senior manager in payments, risk, digital, data or technology at a Canadian bank, credit union or insurer | VP, SVP or C-level in the same functions, at a named institution the others recognise |
| Location | Greater Toronto Area | Same |
| Why they accept | Building a peer network. Genuinely wants the answer to the question. Has fewer invitations and more curiosity | Rarely, and only for the question or for who else is in the room |
| Acceptance rate | Higher | Low, and it should be assumed to be low |
| What they cost us | Research and one approach | Sustained work, a warm route, and often a nomination |
| What they are worth | Fills the room. Becomes an anchor in two years | **Makes the room. One anchor acceptance converts several Level 1 acceptances that would otherwise not have happened** |
| Risk | A room of only Level 1 guests is a room the client will not pay for twice, because the titles do not match the account list | A room built for one anchor who then cancels is a room with nothing holding it together |

**The result to watch for, and it is the inverse of Run A's.** In Run A the harder buyer, Level 2, is the better one. Here the harder guest is also the more valuable one, and for the same structural reason: the difficulty is the value. But the sequencing is opposite. Run A sells to Level 2 first. **Run B must secure Level 2 anchors first and build Level 1 around them**, because the acceptance logic runs downhill and does not run uphill. A Level 1 guest is reassured by an anchor's presence. An anchor is not reassured by three directors.

**Consequence.** The founding guest cohort work in `phase-5-design-partners.md` is anchor-first, and it is slow, and it is the business. There is no fast version of this.
